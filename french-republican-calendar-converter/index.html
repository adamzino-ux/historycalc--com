Historical Context

The French Republican (or Revolutionary) calendar was adopted by the National Convention on 5 October 1793 but made retroactive to 22 September 1792 — the day the First French Republic was proclaimed and, fittingly, the autumn equinox. Mathematician Gilbert Romme designed its decimal structure while poet Fabre d'Églantine invented the poetic month names. The calendar replaced the seven-day week with a ten-day "décade," and even divided each day into 10 decimal hours. Napoleon Bonaparte abolished it effective 1 January 1806, though it briefly reappeared for eighteen days during the Paris Commune of 1871.

Did You Know? Fabre d'Églantine gave every single day of the year its own name tied to a plant, animal, mineral, or farm tool — for example, 1 Vendémiaire is "Grape" and 3 Nivôse is "Cypress." The ten-day décade meant workers got only one rest day every ten days instead of every seven, a major reason the calendar was unpopular. Decimal time (10 hours a day, 100 minutes an hour) was legally required for a year but almost nobody actually re-made their clocks.

How It's Calculated

The converter counts the exact number of days between your chosen date and the calendar's epoch, 22 September 1792 (Republican Year I, Vendémiaire 1), using Julian Day Numbers for precision across any date. Republican years run 365 days, except "sextile" (leap) years of 366 days, which fall — following the mathematical rule proposed by Gilbert Romme and confirmed by the real equinox during the calendar's active years — whenever the following year number is divisible by 4 (skipping century years not divisible by 400, mirroring the Gregorian leap rule). Each ordinary year has 12 months of exactly 30 days, grouped into three 10-day décades, followed by 5 (or 6, in leap years) extra "jours complémentaires" or sansculottides.

Formula: day-count = JDN(date) − JDN(22 Sept 1792), then that count is divided into Republican years (365 or 366 days each) to find the year, month, and day.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Republican years are almost always written as Roman numerals (Year VIII, not "Year 8") in historical texts — remember this when reading primary sources.
  • The calendar has no dates before 22 September 1792; anything earlier (like the storming of the Bastille in 1789) can't be converted and predates the Republic itself.
  • "Sansculottides" are not part of any month — they sit outside the 12×30-day structure as standalone festival days.
  • Because the calendar was abolished in 1805, dates after Year XIV use a mathematical extension of Romme's leap rule, not a historically observed one.
  • Décade days (Primidi–Décadi) replace weekdays entirely — there is no Republican "Monday" or "Sunday."

Frequently Asked Questions

What year is it in the French Republican calendar?

Using the mathematical extension of Romme's leap-year rule, today's date converts to a specific Republican year, month and day — check the "Today's Republican Date" box near the top of this page for the live conversion.

When did the French Republican calendar start and end?

It began retroactively on 22 September 1792 (the day the Republic was proclaimed) and was officially abolished by Napoleon effective 1 January 1806. It reappeared briefly for 18 days during the Paris Commune in 1871.

How many days are in a French Republican month?

Every month has exactly 30 days, split into three ten-day "décades." The 5 or 6 leftover days at year's end are separate festival days called sansculottides, not part of any month.

What are the sansculottides?

They are the extra days added to complete the 365 (or 366, in leap years) day year: Virtue, Genius/Talent, Labor, Opinion, and Rewards, plus a sixth "Revolution" day in leap years.

Why did Napoleon abolish the calendar?

It was unpopular with workers (one rest day per ten instead of seven), disrupted trade with other nations still on the Gregorian calendar, and clashed with the Catholic Church's calendar of feast days, which Napoleon was trying to reconcile with under the 1801 Concordat.

Was the Republican calendar ever accurate to the real equinox?

For its active years (I–XIV), leap years were meant to track the true astronomical autumn equinox at the Paris Observatory. In practice only Years III, VII, and XI were actually observed as leap, matching a simple 4-year rule during that period.
