Why This Matters

The Hebrew calendar governs Jewish holidays, Torah readings, yahrzeits (memorial anniversaries), and Bar/Bat Mitzvah dates, all of which shift relative to the Gregorian calendar every year because Hebrew months follow the moon while the year is anchored to the solar seasons. Genealogists tracing family records from Eastern Europe, historians dating rabbinic responsa or ketubot (marriage contracts), and anyone planning a Passover seder or Rosh Hashanah dinner needs a reliable way to move between the two systems. For example, knowing that 15 Nisan always falls on the first night of Passover only helps if you can translate it into a specific Gregorian date each spring. This tool removes the guesswork by applying the same arithmetic rules used in printed Jewish calendars since the 4th century.

How It's Calculated

The Hebrew calendar is lunisolar: twelve lunar months of 29 or 30 days (about 354 days total) are periodically corrected with a leap month, Adar II, so the calendar stays aligned with the solar year. Leap years occur in years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17, and 19 of a 19-year Metonic cycle — the rule is (7 × year + 1) mod 19 < 7. Each month's length depends on the "molad" (mean lunar conjunction) and postponement rules (dechiyot) that prevent Rosh Hashanah from falling on Sunday, Wednesday, or Friday. This converter implements the classical arithmetic algorithm (delay calculations, month-day tables, and Julian Day Number bridging) to move precisely between the Hebrew epoch of 3761 BCE and any Gregorian date.

Historical Context

The modern fixed-arithmetic Hebrew calendar was formalized around 359 CE, traditionally credited to Sanhedrin president Hillel II, replacing the earlier practice of observing new moons by eyewitness testimony and sending messengers to announce the new month across Jewish communities. Years are counted from Anno Mundi (AM), the traditional date of Creation, placed at 3761 BCE — so the Gregorian year 2025 overlaps mostly with Hebrew year 5785/5786. The calendar's 19-year Metonic cycle, which reconciles 235 lunar months with 19 solar years, was independently discovered by the Greek astronomer Meton of Athens in 432 BCE and by Babylonian astronomers even earlier, showing how ancient cultures converged on the same lunisolar solution.

Did You Know?
  • Because Rosh Hashanah can't fall on Sunday, Wednesday, or Friday, some Hebrew years are "deficient" (353 days), "regular" (354 days), or "complete" (355 days) — and leap versions add exactly 30 days for Adar I.
  • The Hebrew day begins at sunset, not midnight, so a Hebrew date actually spans parts of two Gregorian dates.
  • Adar II, the leap month, is inserted 7 times every 19 years — the same 19-year cycle used independently in ancient Babylon and Classical Greece.
  • Modern equivalent: Hebrew year 5785 means the calendar's epoch (3761 BCE) predates the Gregorian calendar reform of 1582 CE by more than 5,300 years — roughly the same gap that separates today from the invention of writing in ancient Sumer.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Remember the Hebrew day starts at sunset — a date labeled "1 Tishrei" in civil terms corresponds to the evening before through the following daylight.
  • Don't assume every year has the same number of months — leap years have 13 months (Adar I and Adar II), which shifts holiday dates by roughly a month compared to non-leap years.
  • Cheshvan and Kislev vary in length (29 or 30 days) depending on the year type, which is why the day dropdown adjusts automatically for the year and month you pick.
  • This tool uses the proleptic Gregorian calendar for all conversions; dates before 1582 CE in real historical records may have used the Julian calendar instead.
  • Double-check leap year status before scheduling a Hebrew-date anniversary years in advance, since Adar I/Adar II naming can be confusing for Purim and Bar Mitzvah planning.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert a Gregorian date to the Hebrew calendar?

Select the "Gregorian → Hebrew" tab, choose your date using the date picker, and click convert. The tool instantly shows the Hebrew year, month, and day, along with the weekday and whether it's a leap year.

Why does the Hebrew year change in September or October?

The Hebrew year begins on 1 Tishrei (Rosh Hashanah), which usually falls in September or early October on the Gregorian calendar, so each Hebrew year overlaps two consecutive Gregorian years.

What is a Hebrew leap year?

A leap year adds a 13th month, Adar II, to keep the lunar calendar synchronized with the solar seasons. This happens 7 times in every 19-year cycle, following the rule (7×year+1) mod 19 < 7.

How accurate is this Hebrew date converter?

It implements the classical fixed arithmetic rules used in printed Jewish calendars since the calendar's standardization around 359 CE, so it matches published Hebrew calendars for any year, ancient or modern.

Does the Hebrew calendar start at midnight like the Gregorian calendar?

No — Hebrew days begin at sunset. This converter reports the civil (daytime) equivalent date, which is the convention most calendars and software use for date matching.

What year is it on the Hebrew calendar right now?
